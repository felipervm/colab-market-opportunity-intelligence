What the scores mean (and don't mean) + methodology

Product fit = degree to which the existing CoLab design-review/engineering knowledge proposition maps to the segment. Public evidence = strength of named case examples in this limited source set. Impact = hypothesized intensity of complex design review. Sales accessibility = analyst estimate of procurement/onboarding complexity (higher = potentially easier). Testability = feasibility of learning quickly via a narrow research sprint (higher = easier). These judgments require internal validation; they are not market sizes, win rates, or financial forecasts. Weighted sum of 1–5 ratings, divided by total weight. The model does not incorporate pricing, TAM/SAM/SOM, contract value, margins, current pipeline, or implementation effort because none is known.

03 / RECOMMENDATION

Make a small bet.
Design it to learn.

Initial directional recommendation: test adjacent industrial equipment opportunities while using automotive and aerospace as comparison benchmarks. This is a learning priority, not a claim about CoLab's actual GTM roadmap.

WEEK 01

Define the ICP hypothesis

Work with Sales, Product, Marketing, Customer Success and solutions engineers. Define the buyer, review workflow, trigger and measurable pain. Compare the alternatives buyers consider, and ask where CoLab already wins or loses.

WEEK 02

Build a small account sample

Map 10–15 publicly identifiable accounts per lane with evidence of distributed engineering reviews. Verify CAD/PLM context and exclude unsupported assumptions.

WEEKS 03–04

Test against reality

Summarize qualified pain, discovery conversion, competitor mentions, access to decision-makers, deployment complexity, and pilot feasibility in an executive brief. Re-rank only with observed evidence; stop if signals are weak.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND?

If internal pipeline conversion, sales-cycle data, or customer expansion evidence favors automotive or aerospace, I would override this public-data ranking. The purpose of a strategy model is to make assumptions contestable—not to defend a favorite market.
